Creatures

The synth sounds are not patches — they are creatures, each with a character, living in the environment the drums create.

Each idea below has two halves: what it feels like to play with, and how to build it in Live. The creatures are named loosely so they can be referred to in patches and scenes. Their voices are all MiniFreak presets — one synth holds several at once, or one creature per scene.

The turnaroundform-aware — knows where it is in the bar count

What it feels like The environment counts along with you. In 4/4 it knows a four- or eight-bar phrase is coming to its turnaround and, as you approach it, the creatures brace — a pad holds its breath, a pluck leans in. On the downbeat of the new phrase something happens: the key shifts under you, or everything drops out for a bar and comes back changed, or a new creature wakes up.
How to build it
  • Live's transport is the form clock: a Max for Live device reads live.set current bar and counts within an 8-bar phrase.
  • Bars 7–8 raise a "turnaround" signal mapped to tension parameters: pad filter down, delay feedback up, arp rate halving.
  • On bar 1: weighted chance picks an event — transpose, silence (mute one bar), or wake (launch the next clip).
  • Chance weights modulated by playing: high density favours wake, low favours silence.

The surfaceposition-pitched — the head is a keyboard

What it feels like The drumhead is no longer one note. Centre is low and dark, moving outward the note climbs and the tone opens. A roll that drifts from the middle to the rim becomes a rising, brightening phrase. The pad under the sticks becomes a melodic instrument you play by where, not just when.
How to build it
  • Blended zones (Centre ↔ Edge): Timbre outputs a continuous 0–1 position per hit.
  • Max for Live quantises the CC into a scale (5–8 steps) and rewrites the note pitch.
  • Unquantised CC maps to filter cutoff so brightness tracks position.
  • Start with one tom as the surface; expand once the scale feels right.

The mimiclistens, learns the groove, carries it on — and decays without attention

What it feels like You lay down a rhythm and the mimic starts to shadow it — first exactly, then in its own way, keeping the shape but choosing variations. Stop and it keeps going. But leave it too long and it gets thinner. Hits go missing, the timing loosens, the pitch drifts. It is still your idea, but it is forgetting it. Come back in and it brightens. It is a creature you have to keep feeding.
How to build it
  • Capture: Max for Live records incoming drum MIDI into a rolling buffer (last 2–4 bars), storing beat position, velocity, zone and micro-timing.
  • Model: per-step hit probabilities and a velocity profile (first-order Markov chain, weighted by recency).
  • Life meter: 0–1, rises with each real hit (fast attack), decays exponentially when none arrive.
  • Decay behaviours driven by (1 − life): probability drops, timing jitters, pitch drifts, tone degrades. At life ≈ 0, silence.

The understudybass-to-synth handoff — the groove transfers and mutates

What it feels like The bass groove you looped hands off to the synth and it starts to make it its own. It adds notes, leaves some out, finds syncopated bits you did not play. The groove you laid down is still there underneath, but the synth is riffing on it, learning the general feel and playing with it musically.
How to build it
  • Bass audio → Jam Origin MIDI Bass (real-time) → MIDI recorded alongside the audio loop.
  • On turnaround, MIDI routes to MiniFreak; audio crossfades out.
  • Scale MIDI effect keeps additions in key. Note-chance device drops/shifts/adds at probability thresholds.
  • Same groove-learning principle as the mimic: the longer it plays, the further it drifts.

Voice routing

Each creature can be set to listen to any other creature's output — its notes, its dynamics, its life signal. When two creatures listen to each other, they start forming feedback loops.

  • Routing matrix: a Max for Live device or a simple send/receive bus where each creature's output signals (notes fired, velocity profile, life meter, density) are published, and each creature subscribes to whichever others it wants to hear.
  • Response rules: when creature A hears creature B fire a note, it might echo it (transposed), answer it (a complementary rhythm), or go quiet (make space). These rules go both ways — B also reacts to A.
  • Locking in: two creatures listening and responding to each other will eventually converge — their patterns interlock and a repeating figure emerges from what started as generative drift. This is the payoff: emergent structure from simple rules.
  • Spiralling out: or they do not converge. The feedback amplifies differences instead of smoothing them — density escalates, timing scatters, the texture grows chaotic. A life-meter decay or a turnaround event can reset the spiral, or you can let it ride and play into the chaos.
  • Manual routing: switching which voices listen to which could be a performance gesture — a knob or a Push pad that rewires the connections mid-set, changing which creatures are in conversation.

This is where the system stops being reactive and starts being relational. The creatures are not just listening to the drummer — they are listening to each other. The music makes itself.

Where this leads

Creatures that react to the same signals will start to seem to react to each other — the mimic's hits count as "playing" for the surface's filter, the turnaround can put the mimic to sleep. One Max for Live device per creature, with a shared bus of a few signals (life, turnaround, density, position), is enough architecture; the creatures are defined by which signals they listen to and how they answer.
