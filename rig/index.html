Concept

A musical environment to play in, respond to, and be responded to by — three live voices played solo, with a generative layer that listens to how the drums are played and steers the synth.

The three voices are electronic percussion (Sensory Percussion 2 on a compact mesh-head kit), bass guitar, and the MiniFreak. Ableton Live is the backbone for recording, looping, routing and the "intelligence" layer, but it is driven from hardware so the computer stays out of the way during performance. Nothing is a hardware looper; the versatility of Live's session view, Max for Live and MIDI effects is the point.

The reference is the Arthur Hnatek / Jojo Mayer (Nerve, Me/Machine) model: a system that reads dynamics, hit patterns and timing and responds. The method is genre-agnostic — the vocabulary here leans melodic techno rather than jazz, which is a smaller and faster language to become fluent in, and which the simplified kit suits well.

Principles

  • Ableton is the brain, hardware is the hands. Anything touched mid-performance is a knob, pad or pedal.
  • Commit tone early in the analogue chain; keep Live's effects for what is looped, modulated or automated live.
  • Buy toward a problem that has been hit, not toward a spec. The MiniFreak's own sequencer and arpeggiator come first; a hardware step sequencer only if they run out.
  • Fewer voices, more expression per voice. A snare with four trained zones beats eight pads with one each.

Physical layout

Seated centrally. Each instrument sits on one side of the chair so that switching between them is a turn, not a move; the shared controls sit in front.

Audio signal flow

Three independent inputs into one multi-channel interface. Live records and loops each one on its own track before the mix goes to monitors.

The MiniFreak's audio goes in as line level regardless of what is sequencing it. The drums can arrive two ways: as Sensory Percussion's own rendered sound (Portal outputs) or as mics on the mesh heads if a recorded acoustic layer is wanted — one of the open decisions.

Control flow

Separate from audio. Four control sources, one destination.

The effects controller does not need a hardware switch between instruments. Live's "map to selected track" behaviour retargets the same knobs to whichever track is selected, so one modular controller (magnetic snap-together units such as Intech Studio Grid or Monogram Creative Console are candidates) covers drums, synth and bass. Selecting the track can itself be a pad on Push or a footswitch.

Percussion

Sensory Percussion 2 on a compact mesh kit: snare plus two or three toms. Expression comes from zones and technique, not from pad count.

What the sensor reads

It does not read acoustic timbre. What the software calls "timbre" is derived from where and how the head is struck — position and strike classification — not from spectral analysis. The raw axes are position/zone, velocity, and speed (hit rate). Everything else in the expression list is computed from those three.

Each sensor can be trained with up to ten zones per drum: centre, edge, rimshot-centre, rimshot-edge, rim tip, rim shoulder, cross-stick, damped, shell, and a "void" zone for things to ignore. Most players train four to six that they can strike reliably and distinctly; zones can be blended so they flow into each other proportionally rather than switching hard. The built-in controller types are Timbre, Velocity, Speed and LFO, each mappable to any parameter or sent out as MIDI CC.

Bass chain

A clean DI into the interface has no character. Character goes in the analogue chain before the interface, where it is zero-latency and under a hand or foot.

  • No DI box needed if the interface has a Hi-Z (instrument) input — most do, on input 1. A DI only earns its place for a thru to an amp, or built-in tone shaping.
  • Character: Tech21 SansAmp Bass Driver DI is the standard answer to sterile DI bass — amp-style saturation and a DI in one analogue box. A compressor before it (MXR Bass Compressor or similar) evens dynamics, which also keeps loop levels consistent.
  • Digital alternatives are good now (Neural DSP Darkglass is the bass-specific benchmark). Plugin latency is a buffer-size issue, roughly 3–8 ms round trip at 64–128 samples, not inherent to being digital. The argument for pedals here is tactility, not sound quality.

Chain: bass → compressor → SansAmp → interface Hi-Z input → Live. Reserve Live's effects on the bass track for things that are looped or modulated live.

Synth and patterns

The MiniFreak is the right synth for this. It is a step sequencer question that is really a generative-system question.

Six-voice true polyphony (pads and chords work), two oscillator engines per voice across wavetable, FM, harmonic and other types, a modulation matrix, built-in effects, and Spice & Dice randomisation aimed squarely at exploratory sound design. Pads, plucks, bass, stabs, washes and chords all come from one unit. It is hybrid (digital oscillators, analogue-modelled filter), so vintage analogue grit would be a second-synth conversation later, not now.

Patterns

A fixed-grid step sequencer is deterministic; the brief is generative. The MiniFreak already has a polyphonic sequencer and arpeggiator with modulation lanes, both reachable by Spice & Dice. The "responds to what is played" layer lives in Live: probability MIDI effects, Max for Live generative devices, and parameters modulated from Sensory Percussion data. Start there; add a dedicated hardware sequencer only against a limitation actually hit.

First experiment

  1. Hi-hat speed (Sensory Percussion's Speed controller, sent as CC) → MiniFreak filter cutoff via Live's Expression Control. Continuous: busier playing opens the pad.
  2. Snare velocity → a macro on the reverb send of the synth track. Per-hit: harder hits bloom into space.

One continuous mapping, one per-hit mapping, both from stock devices. Once parameters move with playing, add probability and bounded randomisation so the system surprises as well as responds.

Expression mappings

What the playing can do to the synth and effects, from simple and legible to poetic.

1 Direct, one to one

  • Velocity → amplitude of the synth voice
  • Velocity → filter cutoff: harder is brighter
  • Zone → patch or layer: edge is a pluck, centre a pad, rim a stab
  • Speed → cutoff or resonance, continuously
  • Strike type → separate voice: rimshot stabs, open hits swell

2 Shaping rather than switching

  • Velocity → envelope attack/release: hard is snappy, soft is slow
  • Position → pan, mirroring where the stick lands
  • Speed → delay feedback: busy playing builds wash
  • Speed → LFO rate on a pad's tremolo
  • Velocity → reverb send: loud blooms, soft stays dry

3 Derived and relational

  • Micro-timing (ahead/behind the grid) → delay time or LFO phase, so rushing or dragging destabilises the texture
  • Dynamic variance → randomisation amount on the generative sequencer: erratic playing makes the system wilder, steady playing calms it
  • Rolling average velocity → intensity macro that blends in unison or extra oscillator layers as energy builds
  • Position drift across a phrase → wavetable or oscillator blend, giving a phrase an arc rather than a per-hit reaction

4 Poetic — the system has a personality

  • Silence → growth: an ambient layer swells during rests and recedes on re-entry; stopping becomes a compositional act
  • Call and response: sample the last phrase and answer it a bar later, decayed or reharmonised
  • Fatigue: cumulative density over the set slowly darkens or saturates the tone, giving the whole performance a long arc
  • Decaying influence: each hit nudges a parameter and the nudge decays over seconds, leaving a trail like ripples
  • Surprise threshold: unusually steady playing triggers a rare one-off event — a key change, a drop-out, a new voice
  • Cross-modulation between limbs: the time gap between hat and snare hits drives something neither does alone
  • Harmonic memory: recent choices bias what the sequencer may play next, so the piece remembers where it has been

Suggested order after the first experiment: position → timbre blend (expression independent of volume), then dynamic variance → randomisation amount (the first mapping that feels alive rather than reactive).

Creatures

A way of thinking about the synth sounds: not patches but creatures, each with a character, living in the environment the drums create — listening to the playing and to each other.

Each idea below has two halves. The left is what it feels like to play with; the right is how to build it in Live. The creatures are named loosely so they can be referred to in patches and scenes. Their voices are all MiniFreak presets, so one synth can hold several at once (six voices shared between them, or one creature per scene).

The turnaround form-aware — knows where it is in the bar count

The environment counts along with you. In 4/4 it knows a four- or eight-bar phrase is coming to its turnaround and, as you approach it, the creatures brace — a pad holds its breath, a pluck leans in. On the downbeat of the new phrase something happens: the key shifts under you, or everything drops out for a bar and comes back changed, or a new creature wakes up and begins its own process. You stop having to signal section changes; the music arrives at them with you.

  • Live's transport is the form clock: a Max for Live device reads live.set current bar and beat and counts within an 8-bar (configurable) phrase.
  • Bars 7–8 raise a "turnaround" signal (0→1 ramp) mapped to the creatures' tension parameters: pad filter cutoff easing down, delay feedback up, arp rate halving.
  • On bar 1 of the next phrase the device picks one event by weighted chance: transpose (send a MIDI transpose offset to the MiniFreak track via a Pitch MIDI effect, from a small list of related keys), silence (mute group for one bar, then unmute), or wake (launch the next clip in a creature's slot, starting a new modulation sequence).
  • The chance weights are themselves modulated by playing: high recent density favours wake, low density favours silence.
  • Pair with Tempo Follower so the bar count stays yours even without a click.

The surface position-pitched — the head is a keyboard

The drumhead is no longer one note. Striking between centre and edge plays a different pitch and a different brightness: centre is low and dark, moving outward the note climbs and the tone opens. A roll that drifts from the middle to the rim becomes a rising, brightening phrase. The pad under the sticks becomes a melodic instrument you play by where, not just when.

  • In Sensory Percussion, use blended zones (Centre ↔ Edge) so the Timbre controller outputs a continuous 0–1 position value per hit rather than a hard switch.
  • Send Timbre as MIDI CC alongside the hit's note. In Live, a Max for Live device quantises the CC into a scale (e.g. 5–8 steps across a chosen mode) and rewrites the note's pitch before it reaches the MiniFreak track.
  • The same CC, unquantised, maps to the MiniFreak's filter cutoff (CC via Live's Expression Control or a direct MIDI map) so brightness tracks position continuously.
  • Velocity still shapes amplitude and envelope so the surface keeps its drum feel.
  • Start with one tom as the surface and leave the snare as a trigger; expand once the scale feels right.

The mimic listens, learns the groove, carries it on — and decays without attention

You lay down a rhythm and the mimic starts to shadow it — first exactly, then in its own way, keeping the shape and the accents but choosing its own variations. You can stop and it keeps going, as if it had understood. But it lives on your attention: the longer you leave it, the thinner it gets. Hits start to go missing. The timing loosens, the pitch drifts a little, the tone goes slightly wrong. It is still playing your idea, but it is forgetting it. Come back in and it brightens and tightens again. It is a creature you have to keep feeding.

  • Capture: a Max for Live device records incoming drum MIDI into a rolling buffer (last 2–4 bars, aligned to Live's grid), storing for each hit its beat position, velocity, zone and micro-timing offset.
  • Model: build per-step hit probabilities and a velocity profile from the buffer (a first-order Markov chain over steps, weighted by recency). This is "the essence" — accents and density per beat, not a verbatim copy.
  • Continue: each step, fire a note with that probability, velocity drawn from the profile ± a small spread, into the mimic's creature track. Keep the drummer's zone → sound mapping so the mimic plays the same voices.
  • Life meter: a value from 0–1 that rises with each real hit (fast attack) and decays exponentially when none arrive (slow release, tens of seconds).
  • Decay behaviours, all driven by (1 − life): probability floor drops (hits go missing); timing jitter increases (wonkier); pitch and filter cutoff get random walks (drift); sample-rate reduction or detune creeps in (tone goes wrong). At life ≈ 0 the mimic goes silent.
  • Feeding it: real hits refresh the buffer continuously, so the mimic tracks a changing groove, and life snaps back up.

Where this leads

Creatures that react to the same signals will start to seem to react to each other — the mimic's hits can count as "playing" for the surface's filter, the turnaround can put the mimic to sleep. One Max for Live device per creature, with a shared bus of a few signals (life, turnaround, density, position), is enough architecture; the creatures are defined by which signals they listen to and how they answer.

Time and tempo

Playing without a click and letting Live follow is possible. Live 11 introduced a built-in Tempo Follower; before that it was BeatSeeker, a paid Max for Live device from research at Queen Mary's Centre for Digital Music. Either listens to a rhythmic audio signal and moves Live's tempo to match, and switching between fixed and following modes can be mapped to a footswitch.

What it tolerates: subtle fluctuation and ordinary push-and-pull. What it struggles with, by user reports: sustained drift — speeding up or slowing down over several bars — where it can latch onto the initial tempo and let the grid slip away. Deliberately sloppy timing (random jitter) mostly gets smoothed over. This needs testing with the real kit before designing around it; a hybrid where the drums set tempo at the start of a section and the grid holds for the loop may be the practical answer.

Related decision: clock master. If the MiniFreak's sequencer is synced to Live, following tempo moves everything together. If it runs free, loops recorded into Live are unquantised and the feel is looser.

Open decisions

Further research

Areas not yet covered that will shape the build.

Latency budget

End-to-end: sensor → Portal → Live → synth → interface → ears. Set a target (under ~10 ms felt) and measure each link.

MIDI routing and clock

Who sends clock, who receives, and how Portal, MiniFreak, Push and the controller share one USB host without feedback loops.

Max for Live devices to learn

Expression Control, Envelope Follower, LFO, Shaper, MIDI probability tools; which existing devices cover tiers 3–4 before writing patches.

Set structure

How a performance is organised in session view: scenes as sections, how a loop is committed, cleared, or handed to the next section.

Monitoring and room

Low-volume practice: headphone mix, mesh-head noise, neighbours. Whether a small PA is needed at all.

Capturing sessions

Record every session multitrack by default so exploratory playing is kept; how to tag and revisit.

Drumming vocabulary

A melodic-techno drumming language for a snare and toms: patterns, fills, dynamics; a learning path independent of jazz.

Bass role in techno

Sub vs mid bass, octave and filter pedals, how the bass shares low end with the synth.

Power and cabling

One power strip, cable runs that don't cross the seat, USB hub reliability.

Visual feedback

What needs to be seen without a screen: a small display, Push's LEDs, or nothing.
